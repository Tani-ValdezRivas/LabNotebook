**Overwintering Oysters in Massachusetts**

*What are Overwintering Oysters?* 

Overwintering is the process of dormancy that Oysters undergo as water temperatures decline below 5°C. When oysters experience temperatures below 5°C, metabolic changes occur as they have limited capacity to acclimate to colder temperatures, resulting in near or complete valve closure.  

During this time, oysters cease filtration, leading to an interruption in feeding. Thus, this leads to a depletion in their nutrient reserves as they still need to maintain muscular contraction and basic cellular function. As oysters use their energy reserves, such as [^1]lipids and [^2]proteins, this can result in nutrient catabolism. The sodium-potassium (Na+/K+ ATPase) pumps are affected. Oysters experience membrane rigidity, which reduces the activity of the transmembrane protein. To combat this, oysters can remodel their lipid membranes by increasing the proportion of highly unsaturated fatty acids or altering the phospholipid: sterol ratio in a process called homeoviscous adaptation. However, this requires the mobilization of polyunsaturated fatty acids from reserve lipids to the polar lipids of the cell membranes. The reduction in lipids forces oysters to rely on carbohydrates and amino acids, leading to an accumulation of toxic ammonia. These energy expenses are difficult to bear when winter temperatures reduce or stop feeding. 

Some evidence suggests that oyster seeds are sensitive to winter temperature, leading to high mortality, which suggests that smaller or younger oysters do not have energy reserves to survive compared to adult oysters. Additionally, some studies have seen that hatchery-produced seeds in bivalves had higher metabolic rates, leading to a reduction in their ability to conserve energy reserves.  

*Overwintering Methods in Massachusetts*

Typically, in Massachusetts, farmers move their oysters from their farm location to a storage location. Typically known as a seed pit, these areas are cold (30-40º F) and damp (over 90% relative humidity) and oysters are stored for approximately three months. After 15 months, studies have demonstrated that survival starts to decline.  

The following is a comparison of three common methods used to overwinter oyster: 

1. Seed Pit 
    

2. Did not lose growth in the following year after returning on flats for the growing season 
    

3. Reduced fouling, also minimized ice damage 
    

4. Had greater year-one survival  
    

5. Sinking 
    

6. Allowed oysters to grow in shell length during the winter 
    

7. However, overall had the lowest growth in shell length 
    

8. Leaving on Flats 
    

9. Lead to the highest mortality rate as oysters were crushed under the seabed 
    

Over the rest of the year (from March to December), the oysters from each of the overwintering methods survived equally well.

**Changing winters: how overwintering strategy and temperature affect cultured oysters** 

- Oyster aquaculture is critical part of the blue economy in Massachusetts 
    

- Oyster aquaculture is a $28 million industry  
    

- Most farms are small businesses that are owned and independently operated by MA residents 
    

- Growth of the industry is limited by: 
    

- Lack of recommendations tailored to needs of growers in colder waters 
    

- Rapid environment changes 
    

- Rising sea surface temperatures 
    

- In New England, it is rising at a rate of 0.4C per decade 
    

- Oysters often grow for at least two summers 
    

- Winter is a difficult time as extreme temperatures, winter storms, and ice can hinder the product and gear 
    

- Farms often located in shallow water embayment 
    

- Leads to harsh conditions such as freezing or rough winter storms 
    

- Farmers can adapt various overwintering strategies to improve survivability 
    

- Bottom cages 
    

- Water is typically warmer in comparison to surface cahes 
    

- Surface cages 
    

- Pitting 
    

- Usually occurs on farms that grow their oysters intertidally (oysters exposed to air at low side) 
    

- Usually prone to ice cover and scour, which can lead to high mortality 
    

- Stored for 3 months  
    

- Survivability: 90% 
    

- Study showed that oysters had reduced shell growth compared to other overwintering strategies but grew more over the summer 
    

- No other replicate study has been done 
    

- Reduces exposure external parasites since they are unable to survive extend periods out of water 
    

- Water is warmer compared to surface and bottom cage overwintering strategies  
    

- Limited research on which overwintering strategies would yield best results for growers 
    

- Environmental conditions experienced in one season can carry over to impact performance in the following season  
    

- Summer: exposure to warm temperatures early in the summer affects tissue and shell growth later in the summer 
    

- Winter: Late winter temperature influence prevalence and intensity of Perkinsus in the following fall 
    

- Limited information regarding marinus, boring sponge, mud blister worms, and MSX 
    

- In regard to parasites: 
    

- Temperature is positively correlated with P. marinus and H. nelsoni in terms of prevalence and intensity 
    

- Temperature is negatively correlated with Polydora spp.  
    

- Minimal studies regarding rising impacting of winter warming 
    

- Temperature increased around + 2C during compared to + 0.8C during other seasons 
    

- This can be critical as oysters undergo physiological changes during the winter that shows/ceases respiration and growth 
    

- Warm spells during the winter can reduce the amount of time they are dormant (result in more awakening timepoints) and use energy reserves meant for when food is minimal 
    

- Smaller oysters may be less resilient to warming winter temperature 
    

- Larger parasite load may occur 
    

- The proposal is set to quantify the impact of overwintering husbandry practices and environmental conditions on oyster survival, growth, and parasite load 
    

- Overwintering strategies affect oyster growth and health during the growing season on farms  
    

- Wintertime water temperature affect oyster growth and health during the growing season on farms 
    

- Explore how winter and summertime temperatures differ between overwintering strategies and farms and how they contribute to changes in oyster performance and health 
    

- Quantify oyster survival, tissue and shell growth, and prevalance and intensity of common parasites that causes diseases and/or reduce marketability

- Statement from UMass Lowell: 
    

- Our proposal will quantify the impact of overwintering husbandry practices and environmental conditions on oyster survival, growth, and parasite load. Our specific objectives are to explore how 1) overwintering strategy and 2) wintertime water temperatures affect oyster growth and health during the growing season on farms in Massachusetts. We will conduct a replicated experiment at three collaborating oyster farms in Massachusetts to deploy oysters in the field using the three established overwintering methods to quantify effects of overwintering practice. We will also explore how winter and summertime temperatures differ between overwintering strategies and farms and how they contribute to changes in oyster performance and health. UMass Lowell researchers will be responsible for coordinating and conducting the fieldwork at the North Shore oyster farm (Great Marsh Shellfish Company). We will be the primary point of contact for this farm and be responsible for all experimental deployment, monitoring, maintenance, and data collection for oysters at this farms (Aim 1) and the temperature loggers at these farms for Aim 2. We  will meet with Great Marsh monthly during the overwintering period and Biweekly during the growing season. During the summer, this will involve trips by the UMass Lowell team to the farm to help maintain gear. In addition, the UMass Lowell team will be responsible for screening all oysters for parasites. Specifically, we will determine prevalence and intensity of each of our four parasites using standard methods. C. celata intensity and prevalence on the outside of the oyster shell will be measured for all oysters using the photos taken above, with ImageJ macros. To measure the three other parasites (P. marinus, Polydora spp., and Haplosporidium spp.), we will sacrifice a subset of oysters at each time point (pre-overwintering: n = 30 per size class, N = 60; post-overwintering and post-summer growing: n = 30 per size class per overwintering treatment per farm, N = 540). These oysters will be shucked, and general condition will be noted (color, fullness, gonad size). Samples of mantle, gill, and digestive gland will be removed and saved in RNA later for potential future genetic analyses (see below). Next, a standardized piece of mantle andrectum will be taken for measurement of P. marinus Fluid Thioglycollate Diagnostic method. Oysters will be given a combined intensity score using the Mackin scale (0-5). The rest of the oyster tissue will be embedded in paraffin, sectioned, mounted, stained with Hematoxylin & Eosin, and screened for 1) any structural abnormalities and 2) Haplosporidium spp. infections. If Haplosporidiu spp. infections are found, we will seek additional funds from other sources to perform diagnostic PCR on the saved tissue specimens to determine whether infections are caused by H. nelsoni or the related H. costale (the cause of SSO). Finally, photographs will be taken of the inside of the shells and prevalence and intensity of mud blisters caused by Polydora spp. will be determined using Image J. We expect to primarily find infections by Polydora websterii, but will save representative samples for morphological identification. We will be responsible for analysis of the resulting parasite data and writing up these data for publication.
